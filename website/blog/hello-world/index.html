We built Know Your Code so you can talk through the code you shipped until it makes sense. Before building more, we went looking for where people get stuck. Here's what we found.

Know Your Code is a set of skills for AI coding assistants. Ask about something your app does, like “what happens when I press Save?”, and it follows your code and explains the path out loud. The files, and a way to check the answer yourself, arrive in writing alongside.

It started as one skill for Codex. It now runs in Codex, Cursor, and Claude Code, and it has grown to eight skills. They map a new project, explain a pull request, and find where new code belongs. A few exist to stop you and your assistant from talking past each other.

This is our first post. Instead of a feature list, here's the research shaping what we build next.

01Most of programming is reading

58%

of professional developers' time goes to understanding code, not writing it.

78 developers, 3,148 hours tracked · Xia et al.

Juniors spend a bigger share of their time on it than seniors. If you vibe coded your app, you're somewhere odd. You shipped it, but you didn't write it, so every question starts from zero.

02The question changes as your code grows

One pattern kept showing up across the studies. The size of your codebase changes the question you need answered.

  1. Small · Tens of files “Where does this happen?”A solo project, often built with AI. You don't know where the code lives, so it's hard to tell whether a fix worked.
  2. Medium · Hundreds “What else does this touch?”A few contributors, shared helpers. A fix for one feature breaks another that shares the same code, and nobody notices until later.
  3. Large · A million lines “Why is it like this, and what can't I break?”Many teams, years of history. The rules aren't written down, and the answers are scattered across docs, history, and people.

The middle question is the sneaky one. Researchers call it a reachability question. What can reach this line, and what will a change here reach? Developers ask these all day, and they find them hard.

82%

of developers rated at least one reachability question as hard to answer. They were asking them more than nine times a day.

Survey of 460 developers · LaToza and Myers

The same research found that experienced developers follow the connections in code. Newer ones follow surface clues and spend a lot of time reading code that turns out not to matter (Ko). Newer developers also tend not to ask for help, out of fear of looking incompetent. An assistant you can ask the “obvious” question fixes a small part of a real problem.

03AI help gets weaker where you need it most

The bigger the codebase, the more you'd want help. That's also where AI struggles.

19%SLOWER, MEASURED 20%FASTER, AS THEY FELT IT

Experienced developers working in their own large repositories took longer with AI tools, and believed the opposite.

Randomized trial, 246 tasks · METR

The repositories in that trial averaged over a million lines. The researchers point to large, complex codebases and to tacit knowledge the AI doesn't have. Language models also lose track of information in the middle of a long input (Liu et al.), and a big codebase puts most of what matters in the middle.

At the small end, people building with AI describe a different trap. The model misreads the request, then offers the same failed fix again and again.

“Vibe coding is one thing, vibe debugging is chaos.”A developer quoted in Pimenova et al.

A 2026 survey of 162 vibe coders found that awareness of the risks is widespread, but the ability to check AI code still depends on experience. Knowing a fix might be wrong doesn't help much if you can't tell whether it is.

04Explaining code out loud is its own craft

We're voice-first, so we looked closely at people who already work with code by ear. Developers who use screen readers have been doing it for years.

Their biggest reported challenge is navigation, especially finding something elsewhere without losing your place (HACI review). Audio is linear, so you can't skim ahead (AudioHighlight). Long AI replies are hard to follow by ear, and a code change that mixes added and removed lines is harder still (screen reader study).

Voice interface research adds one more rule. Spoken menus work best at three or four options, and people remember the last one best (Voice User Interface Design).

The second answer is longer, but you can follow it without seeing the screen. It says how many steps are coming, keeps each step to one idea, and ends on the one thing to remember.

05What we changed

These three shipped alongside this post, in the skills repository.

  • Match the size of your project. A small project gets a direct trace. A medium one gets a check on what else touches the code. A large one gets a map first, with a plain note of what was and wasn't searched.
  • Check what else is affected before calling a fix safe. Before changing anything, it names the other code that calls or reads what it's about to touch.
  • Signpost out loud. It opens with how many steps are coming and where they start. Spoken lists stay at three items. When you interrupt, it tells you where you were before answering, so the way back is easy.

The test project from Fig. 1 is now part of our test suite. The new version has to spot the shared helper before touching anything, and the check fails if Search or Export changes.

06What's next

These follow once the first three have been tested in live sessions.

  • Describe changes by ear. Before and after, one behavior at a time, with a count up front, instead of reading a diff aloud.
  • Checks in plain words. Every check says what you'll see if the fix worked, and what you'll see if it didn't.
  • Break the loop. After two failed attempts at the same fix, stop and diagnose instead of trying a third variation.
  • Save a checkpoint first. Suggest a commit before a change that touches many files, so a bad change is easy to undo.

07Try it

Know Your Code is free and open source under the MIT license. Install it for Codex, Cursor, or Claude Code, open your project, and ask something you've been meaning to ask.

  • “What happens when I press Save?”
  • “My changes disappear on refresh. Why?”
  • “I just cloned this. Where do I start?”
